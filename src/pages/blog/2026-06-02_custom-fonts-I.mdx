I had a brief foray into something I've always been interested in: font-making. It proved to be a lot more difficult (and time-consuming) than I was expecting.

Quick shoutout to [Linus Boman](https://www.youtube.com/linusboman) -- I've watched a lot of his videos critiquing fonts used in brand marketing.

# handwriting

Last autumn, I decided I wanted to make a font out of my usual handwriting. Newly equipped with an iPad for university, I spent a few hours quickly making a handwriting font using [Calligraphr](https://www.calligraphr.com/en/)'s base template and [GoodNotes](https://www.goodnotes.com/). The whole process was pretty simple -- I just downloaded the template from Calligraphr, wrote out all the characters, uploaded it, and downloaded the `.ttf` file (which I later converted to `.woff2` format for my website). It's called **Tapioca** because why not be whimsical when choosing the names of my own fonts?

# cursive

I've been doing calligraphy for years (see examples below), so I thought this would be fun, and I could end up with a nice aesthetic calligraphy font to use. _Oh, how wrong I was._

Getting letters to look consistent is extremely difficult, especially when I'm used to writing calligraphy as an entire line, not as individual letters (I mean, that's basically the [whole point of cursive](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cursive#Origin)). My letters have very inconsistent slope, height, and spacing... the font just does not look cohesive. :( I have noticed that generally, cursive fonts have more of a slant, so I'll probably try to implement that on my next cursive font iteration.

I used Calligraphr again for the base template and spent an hour or so writing out the individual characters on my iPad. Then after downloading the `.ttf` file, I saw that the letter spacing wasn't reduced enough, so I used [Glyphr Studio](https://www.glyphrstudio.com/) to further reduce the letter spacing until they mostly connected.
